# Beyond Moore's Law

### How Semiconductor Engineering Is Scaling After Transistor Shrinkage

**Authors:** Heer Thakker • Dhruv Vaghela  
**Branch:** Electronics and Communication Engineering

---

## About the Article

Moore's Law has been one of the defining ideas in semiconductor engineering. Introduced by Gordon Moore in 1965, it described the rapid growth in the number of transistors that could be economically integrated onto a chip.

For decades, transistor scaling delivered improvements in computing performance, power efficiency, and cost. However, the physical and economic assumptions behind traditional scaling have increasingly weakened.

This article explores how the semiconductor industry is continuing to pursue performance improvements as simple transistor shrinkage becomes increasingly difficult.

## Topics Covered

- The origins and significance of Moore's Law
- Dennard scaling and why it enabled decades of performance growth
- The breakdown of Dennard scaling and the power wall
- Physical limitations of nanoscale transistors
- The transition from planar MOSFETs to FinFET and Gate-All-Around architectures
- The growing economic cost of advanced semiconductor manufacturing
- Chiplets and 3D integration as new scaling approaches
- Interconnect and memory bottlenecks
- The impact of AI on semiconductor architecture and EDA
- Emerging technologies beyond conventional transistor scaling

## Key Idea

Moore's Law may be slowing in its traditional transistor-counting form, but semiconductor scaling itself is not stopping.

Instead, performance growth is increasingly coming from multiple engineering directions:

**Device Scaling → Advanced Packaging → 3D Integration → Specialized Architectures → New Materials → AI-Assisted Design**

The future of semiconductor engineering is therefore becoming increasingly heterogeneous, with system-level innovation complementing transistor-level scaling.
